Lesson 2: Error Analysis — Is theta3's Error One Failure or Two?

~10 min · Mission: minimizing error in MDN3R3D

Lesson 1 settled the training question: your train_loss/val_loss track together almost perfectly, with only a mild, well-controlled variance uptick right at the end that early stopping correctly caught. That means the composite loss (NLL + λ·FK) is about as low as this training setup will get.

And yet the model misses your thesis's own bar — mean angle error < 5°, end-effector distance < 5 mm — by a wide margin, almost entirely because of theta3 (38.8° MAE vs. 1.5° for theta1). When the loss curve says "done" but the metric that actually matters says "not even close," more training won't help. The next move isn't a hyperparameter — it's error analysis: looking directly at which examples are wrong and why, before touching the model at all.

Primary source: Andrew Ng, Structuring Machine Learning Projects (Course 3), Week 1 — "Carrying Out Error Analysis." The core idea: before redesigning anything, manually inspect a sample of your errors and count how many fall into each candidate cause. Whichever cause explains the most errors is the one worth fixing first.

Two candidate causes, one symptom

Your FK equations (r = L1 + L2·cos(theta2) + L3·cos(theta2+theta3), z = L2·sin(theta2) + L3·sin(theta2+theta3)) are the classic 2-link planar arm, solved via the law of cosines: 

cos(theta3) = (r² + z² - L2² - L3²) / (2·L2·L3)

This closed form has two properties that can each independently produce a large theta3 error, and they call for completely different fixes:

CauseWhat it isWhat it looks like in your data
Mode confusion Every reachable point has two valid solutions (elbow-up / elbow-down — ±theta3). The mixture picks the wrong branch, or blends both into a value near neither. theta3_pred vs theta3_true scatter shows a second band offset from the diagonal, not scattered noise around it.
Ill-conditioning acos's derivative is -1/sin(theta3) — it blows up as theta3 → 0 or ±pi, i.e. near full stretch or full fold of the arm (the edge of the reachable workspace). Error magnitude correlates with proximity to the workspace boundary, regardless of which branch was picked.

These aren't mutually exclusive — a point near the boundary is also where the two branches are closest together, so confusion and ill-conditioning can compound. Error analysis is how you find out which one is doing most of the damage.

Check yourself

1. You plot theta3_pred against theta3_true for the test set. You see two clean parallel lines, one along the diagonal and one clearly offset below it — not a scattered cloud. What does that indicate?
2. Instead, the scatter is a single noisy cloud around the diagonal, but the noise gets much wider specifically for samples where cos(theta3) is near +-1. What does that indicate?
3. In prediction_summary_dataframe, max_mixture_probability is consistently high (>0.9) even on the samples with the worst theta3 error. What does that rule out?

Question 3 matters because it separates "the model doesn't know which branch" (low confidence, fixable by better mixture separation) from "the model is confidently wrong" (high confidence, points at ill-conditioning or a labeling issue instead).

Your task

Run the error analysis. Your notebook already computes everything needed — call save_prediction_tables(...) on the test set (or reuse it if already saved) to get test_prediction_summary.csv, which has x, y, z, theta3_true, theta3_pred, best_component, max_mixture_probability, selected_component_probability per sample. Then:
  • Scatter: theta3_pred vs theta3_true. Look for a second offset band vs. a single noisy cloud.
  • Boundary check: compute cos_theta3 = (x²+y²+z²-adjustments... ) — more simply, recompute r from x,y and correlate theta3_abs_error against abs(cos(theta3_true)) (closer to 1 = nearer the boundary). A rising trend implicates ill-conditioning.
  • Confidence check: is max_mixture_probability low (mixture genuinely torn) or high (confidently wrong) on the worst-error rows?
Bring back which pattern you see — that determines whether the fix is a representation change (e.g. predicting sin/cos(theta3) instead of raw radians, or a multi-head/flow architecture that separates branches explicitly) or a data-weighting change (oversample near the workspace boundary).
Not sure how to read the scatter, or the pattern looks like neither case? Bring the plot (or just describe what you see) to your teacher directly — that ambiguity is itself useful information.
